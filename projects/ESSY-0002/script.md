# ESSY-0002

# When the Future Suddenly Becomes Visible

## N001

My father had already gone to bed when he remembered that he still needed to take his medication.

Normally, this would have been nothing. Get out of bed, take the pills, lie down again.

But this time, he struggled to get up.

And when he finally did, he couldn't seem to sit properly. For a few moments, his body simply wasn't doing what he wanted it to do.

## N002

In the moment, there wasn't much time to think.

You don't become philosophical when something like that happens. You just deal with whatever is in front of you.

Help him sit up. Get the medication. Make sure he takes it. Help him lie down again.

And then, strangely, things seemed to get better.

## N003

After lying down for a while, he looked more like himself again. The immediate problem had passed.

But that was when the fear really began.

Once everything was quiet, I started replaying those few minutes in my head.

What frightened me was not only what had happened.

It was the thought that this probably wouldn't be the last time.

## N004

We all know that our parents will grow old.

It is one of those facts that is so obvious that we rarely have to think about it.

For most of our lives, aging happens slowly enough to remain almost invisible. A little less energy. A little more medication. Walking becomes slower.

Each change, by itself, can seem manageable.

## N005

And then there are moments when all those gradual changes suddenly become visible at once.

Someone cannot stand up. Someone falls. Someone forgets something they would never have forgotten before.

Someone who has always taken care of other people suddenly needs help with something very basic.

Nothing necessarily changes in that exact moment.

But your understanding of the future does.

## N006

I think much of ordinary life depends on a quiet assumption that tomorrow will be reasonably similar to today.

Intellectually, we know this isn't guaranteed. People get sick. Bodies age. Circumstances change.

But we can't wake up every morning thinking seriously about everything that might go wrong.

So most of the time, we don't.

## N007

We make plans.

We go to work. We think about next year. We imagine retirement. We think about things we might do someday, when we have more time.

There is a kind of stability in assuming that the basic structure of life will remain intact.

Until something happens that makes that assumption harder to maintain.

## N008

When a parent begins to lose some independence, the natural response is to start looking for solutions.

What kind of care do they need? Can the home be made safer? Should someone visit more often? Would professional care be better?

And how do you fit all of this around jobs, children, money, distance, and the rest of ordinary life?

## N009

These are practical questions, and they matter.

But the more carefully you examine them, the more you realize there may be no arrangement in which everything works out well.

More care requires more resources. Keeping someone at home may preserve familiarity, but it may also become unsafe. Professional care solves some problems while creating others.

And the person being cared for may not agree with any of your solutions.

You keep looking for the best option.

Eventually, you begin to wonder whether there is one.

Maybe there are only trade-offs.

## N010

This is uncomfortable for anyone who is used to solving problems.

You gather information, compare alternatives, and try to improve the situation.

But aging doesn't quite behave like that.

You can improve the care. You can reduce risks. You can prepare.

But you cannot solve the underlying problem.

The direction of travel remains the same.

## N011

There is an old Buddhist idea that seems almost too obvious in ordinary language: everything changes.

Impermanence.

The difficult part may not be understanding it.

The difficult part is noticing how much of our emotional life depends on pretending that certain things won't change very much.

## N012

Our parents will still be there.

They will still live in the same house. They will still be able to do the things they have always done.

And then reality begins correcting those assumptions, one at a time.

Psychology has a useful term for some of what follows: anticipatory grief.

Grief does not always begin after something has been lost.

Sometimes it begins when we first understand that a loss is coming.

## N013

That may explain those quiet moments after a small crisis has passed.

Nothing terrible is happening anymore.

The room is calm. The person you were worried about is resting.

And yet you feel worse than you did ten minutes earlier.

Because your mind has already moved beyond the present.

## N014

The obvious response is to prepare.

And preparation matters. Families should have conversations before emergencies force them to. Practical arrangements, money and medical information all matter.

But preparation has a limit.

At some point, preparing for the future can become an attempt to make the future stop being uncertain.

No amount of planning can do that.

## N015

Perhaps this is one reason caring for aging parents can feel so overwhelming.

It reveals the limits of responsibility.

You can be responsible without being in control.

You can care deeply about someone without being able to protect them from everything.

And you can make a thoughtful decision while still disliking what that decision costs.

Love eventually meets limited time, limited money, limited energy, and a body that continues to age.

## N016

Otherwise, responsibility can quietly expand until it becomes impossible.

If something goes wrong, perhaps I should have prevented it.

If I continue working, perhaps I am not giving enough. If I give more, perhaps I am neglecting another part of my life.

There is almost no decision that cannot produce guilt.

Maybe the problem is the assumption that somewhere among all the difficult options, there must be one that leaves everyone safe, happy, and without regret.

There may not be.

## N017

That sounds pessimistic, but I don't think it has to be.

There is a difference between giving up and giving up the demand for a perfect outcome.

We can still make the house safer. We can ask for help. We can have difficult conversations. We can change plans when circumstances change.

But perhaps we don't have to solve the next five years tonight.

## N018

That evening, after my father had taken his medication, he went back to bed.

After a while, he seemed better.

The house became quiet again.

From the outside, very little had happened. There was no dramatic diagnosis. No major decision was made.

But something had changed.

I had seen, for a few minutes, a version of the future that I knew existed but had preferred to keep abstract.

Maybe this is how many changes in life first arrive.

Not as clear turning points.

Not with enough information to tell us what to do next.

Just a small moment in an otherwise ordinary day that makes the future briefly visible.

Then the moment passes.

The future becomes uncertain again.

But once you have seen it, even for a few minutes, it becomes a little harder to pretend you don't know it's coming.
